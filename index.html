Mabrook

A Dubai super app for video, live, music, games, shopping, hotels and an AI assistant. I led design as the product grew from a scattered MVP into one system.

Head of Design

Before my team joined, Mabrook had spent a year in slow development. Much of the UI was AI-generated and inconsistent, and every new vertical arrived with its own patterns.

With food, hotels, shopping, music, games and live streams in one app, the real problem was navigation and a design system that could keep scaling as features kept coming.

  • Set up the design process from scratch: critique, reviews and handoff to Flutter engineers.
  • Led 3 product designers plus marketing graphic and motion designers; personally hired a senior product designer.
  • Joined strategy and roadmap meetings with the C-level team while the product team grew from 15 to 32 people.

  1. Reset the foundationRebuilt the visual style and many core features almost from scratch instead of patching the AI-generated UI.
  2. One feed for every verticalA single full-screen feed with 14 top tabs, one global search and one contextual action: Order now, Book now, Buy or Subscribe.
  3. One checkoutGoods, food and stays share one flow, with a 15-minute booking hold and three ways to pay.
  4. One creation pathPost, reel, podcast, story and live start from the same + button and share one short path to publish.
  5. Retention through playCoins for opening the app, an in-app currency, leaderboards, referral codes and weekly music battles.
  6. A system that scales25 component sets, 470 variants and states, 160 icons, with AI-assisted design cutting feature releases from 10 to 4 days.

The AI assistant sits in the centre of the tab bar: voice or text, in the user’s language, with answers read aloud. A safety layer is on the roadmap: 3 age modes, a 3-hour daily feed limit and 18+ for DMs and live.

Inside the team, AI-assisted design took a feature from brief to release in 4 days instead of 10.

  • Monthly active users grew 4×.
  • Day-7 retention rose from 9% to 14%, day-30 from 3% to 5%.
  • Daily stickiness (DAU/MAU) rose from 11% to 19%: almost one in five monthly users now opens the app every day.
  • 14+ verticals run on one navigation pattern and one checkout.

ASI Central · ESP+

The B2B platform behind North America’s $27.7B promotional products industry. I designed Print-on-Demand, built on the Cosmos design system and helped the team move from mockups to prototypes in code.

ASI has run the promotional products business since 1962. ESP+ grew through acquired modules, three generations of front-end framework and a design language that drifted every time a team shipped in a hurry. The job was to keep shipping new features without adding to the drift.

  • Distributors bought blank products in ESP+, then left to decorate them in one-click print-on-demand services.
  • One product spread across 14 areas: search, orders, quotes and invoices, CRM, collections, presentations, websites, stores, analytics and more.
  • Two brands, ESP+ and ASI Central, on one inconsistent component library.
  • Details surfaced late: missing states in review round three, limits found in QA, components that didn’t exist on handoff day.

  • Designed Print-on-Demand end to end, contributed to the Cosmos design system and helped move the team from static mockups to AI-built prototypes in code.
  • Grew the design team: ran interviews and onboarding for 3 designers I brought in, and helped backfill roles when designers and our UX researcher moved on, so delivery didn’t stall.
  • Worked in a design team led by a VP of Design, alongside PMs, front-end and back-end engineers and QA across 14 product areas.

POD is a fast-growing market (about $13B in 2025, ~26% a year), and we were losing the decoration step to single-purpose tools. Distributor interviews kept coming back to the same line: they bought blanks in ESP+ and printed somewhere else.

  1. Pick a POD-ready productStart from the same catalogue and order.
  2. Place artworkArtwork, text or uploads on the front and back.
  3. Choose method and coloursDecoration method and colour management in one panel.
  4. Set price and marginA price grid and margin, with no second tool and no re-entering data.

Cosmos 1.2 powers both ESP+ and ASI Central: 290+ component sets, 5,300+ variants and states and 500+ design tokens. I helped build and document its components, states and page patterns, so Search, Orders, CRM, Collections, Presentations and POD feel like one product.

Before bringing AI in, we spent six weeks cleaning up the library, so there was something worth generating from.

  1. BriefProduct, design, front-end and QA agree on states, data and a closed list of components in one short session.
  2. Claude and Figma MCPClaude reads the Cosmos library in Figma and builds only from components that already exist.
  3. Figma and GitHubOne run gives Figma frames for every state and a clickable prototype on a GitHub branch.
  4. EngineeringDevelopers keep the prototype’s front end and wire up data, auth and tests.

We kept Figma on purpose: it stays the lasting, reviewable record, and designers review, edit and sign off every generated screen. Read the full story on Medium.

  1. ResearchInterviews with distributors and suppliers.
  2. HypothesisWhat we believe and how we’ll know.
  3. CritiqueDesign critique rounds with the team.
  4. PrototypeClickable, in code, on Cosmos.
  5. ValidateTest with users and stakeholders.
  6. ShipHand off to developers, then measure.

One product team, six features in a quarter:

  • Brief to clickable prototype: 11 → 3.5 days.
  • Review rounds per feature: 3.4 → 1.3.
  • Rework after handoff: 4.2 → 1.1 developer-days.
  • Defects per feature set: 19 → 7.
  • Requirement changes after sign-off: −73% (5.1 → 1.4).
  • Zero off-system components in generated UI.

POD gave distributors a reason to keep decoration inside ESP+. Cosmos gave two brands one consistent language. And the team’s role shifted from drawing screens to engineering prototypes, without giving up Figma as the source of truth.

Uride

Rider and Driver apps for a Canadian intercity ride-share network where drivers work in shifts. Designed from zero to replace a web service; 1M+ rides across Ontario and BC.

2×completed driver sign-ups
2:40 → 0:55time to book an intercity ride

35 → 9 minto submit driver documents

Uride focuses on trips between cities. Unlike typical gig platforms, its drivers are part of the fleet and work scheduled shifts. Backed by private investment and Canadian government grants, it ran on the web: riders booked there and every driver signed up through a long web form.

Driver supply was the real bottleneck. Interviews and funnel data showed where it broke:

  • An 8-step web sign-up with 40+ typed fields and 8 document uploads.
  • Licence, work eligibility, driver’s abstract, background check, vehicle ownership, insurance and safety, with rules that differ by province.
  • No save and resume, so most drop-offs happened at document upload.
  • Drivers work planned shifts, so one no-show breaks a trip between cities.
  • Pay questions were the top support topic.

  • One of two product designers who shaped the product from zero, and later the only designer owning design end to end.
  • Drove the driver side from the start: onboarding with OCR and automated checks, shifts and earnings. Co-created the Rider app and the shared design system.
  • Worked directly with product owners, a project manager who had previously managed product at Uber, and company leadership, turning business goals into flows, prototypes and specs that engineering could ship.
  • Cross-functional team: product owners, the project manager, two product designers, iOS, Android and backend engineers, and QA.

Riders book, track and pay. Drivers onboard, plan shifts, take rides and get paid. Both apps share one system and one ride model, so every state a rider sees (searching, driver on the way, arrived, finished) has its pair on the driver side.

  1. One checklistThe web form became a document checklist, with progress saved after every step.
  2. Scan, don’t typeDrivers photograph their licence and documents.
  3. Data pre-filledOCR and public registries fill in the data; the driver only checks and confirms.
  4. Clear review statusA visible status replaced “we’ll email you”.

  • A daily goal in hours, with progress for every shift.
  • Own availability: preferred, unavailable and flexible time slots.
  • “Can’t make it?” with a reason, so dispatch can re-plan supply instead of discovering a no-show.
  • Personal goals with a guaranteed bonus for reliability: hours online, shift completion, acceptance rate.

Weekly earnings, instant withdraw, auto payouts and achievements made pay easy to understand and turned retention into a product feature.

  1. Route and fixed priceThe fare is clear before booking.
  2. Live matchingRiders see the search and estimated pickup time.
  3. Driver on the wayCar, plate and driver details with live ETA.
  4. One-tap tipPreset amounts or a custom tip.
  5. Scheduled tripsBook longer rides between cities ahead of time.

One visual language for both apps: a Google Sans type scale, a Flame Red core palette with 30 colour styles, 170+ icons and components with every state. 320+ screens across 80+ feature flows, from sign-up to payout.

First 6 months after launch:

  • 2× completed driver sign-ups (31% → 64%), with 70% less manual input.
  • Time to submit documents: 35 → 9 minutes.
  • +38% active drivers, −24% driver churn over 90 days.
  • Shift no-shows −32%, with 94% of shifts covered on time.
  • Average time to book a ride: 2:40 → 0:55, and +18% more requests turned into completed rides.
  • −41% pay-related support tickets.

Uride moved from a web-only service to native apps in both stores. The shared design system let the team ship new flows without new UI and supported the expansion to South America. In 2025 Uride ranked #34 in the Deloitte Technology Fast 50 Canada, with 883% growth over four years.

How I lead design

The products above came from teams, not from one person. This is how I build and run them.

I’ve hired and onboarded 12 designers onto client projects. At its largest my team was 6 designers working on up to 4 products at the same time.

I mentor the team and built career paths and growth plans for mid and senior designers, so people know what the next level looks like and how to get there.

  • Regular design critique and day-to-day design reviews.
  • Design QA before release, so what ships matches what was designed.
  • Documentation standards and a structured handoff to engineering.

I own design system strategy across products: audit what exists, consolidate it, set contribution and governance rules, and get multiple teams to adopt it. Examples: Cosmos at ASI (two brands, one system), SAP’s pilot system, and Mabrook’s system for 14+ verticals.

I base design decisions on evidence: 72 user and stakeholder interviews, hypotheses, usability testing and product analytics. Every case on this page has a metric because the team agreed on it before designing.

I introduced AI into research synthesis, rapid prototyping and documentation for small teams and enterprise clients, and wrote two articles about it. The team uses Claude, Cursor, ChatGPT, Codex, Figma AI and Figma Make, with Claude and Codex connected to Figma.

Figma stays the parallel, safe source of design artifacts for security and backups, so speed never costs control.

I represent design in presale and strategy discussions with client product owners and leadership, scope and estimate new projects, and align design, product and engineering around user and business goals.
