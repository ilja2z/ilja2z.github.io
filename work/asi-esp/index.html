ASI has run the promotional products business since 1962. ESP+ grew through acquired modules, three generations of front-end framework and a design language that drifted every time a team shipped in a hurry. The job was to keep shipping new features without adding to the drift.

  • Distributors bought blank products in ESP+, then left to decorate them in one-click print-on-demand services.
  • One product spread across 14 areas: search, orders, quotes and invoices, CRM, collections, presentations, websites, stores, analytics and more.
  • Two brands, ESP+ and ASI Central, on one inconsistent component library. Code was ahead of design: engineers had a solid component library in Storybook, while Cosmos had only recently moved from Sketch to Figma, many files were broken copies of old Sketch work, and only about half of the components had a green “ready” status.
  • Details surfaced late: missing states in review round three, limits found in QA, components that didn’t exist on handoff day.

  • Designed web stores and presentations, including fundraising for stores. Worked on Print-on-Demand, which wasn’t finished while I was on the project. Contributed to the Cosmos design system and helped move the team from static mockups to AI-built prototypes in code.
  • Grew the design team: ran interviews and onboarding for 3 designers I brought in, and helped backfill roles when designers and our UX researcher moved on, so delivery didn’t stall.
  • Worked in a design team led by a VP of Design, alongside PMs, front-end and back-end engineers and QA across 14 product areas.
  • Options, not one answer. For most features I brought several options to the weekly design review with the whole product team, explained how I got to each and which problem it solved, and we chose together.

POD is a fast-growing market (about $13B in 2025, ~26% a year), and we were losing the decoration step to single-purpose tools. Distributor interviews kept coming back to the same line: they bought blanks in ESP+ and printed somewhere else.

I worked on the first version of this flow. It wasn’t finished while I was on the project, so I show it as work in progress, not as a shipped feature.

When we built fundraising for stores, our PM suggested adding it as a seventh step in the six-step store creation wizard. Data, which reached me through our VP of Design, showed that stores are created often. Fundraising was new, so how many stores would use it could not be measured yet: my judgement from knowing the product was that it would be a small share.

Hypothesis: a new step would slow down everyone to serve the few who need it. We kept the wizard at six steps, and fundraising became a toggle in store settings: a name, a goal and a payout rule, with the fund and its progress shown on the storefront.

Cosmos 1.2 powers both ESP+ and ASI Central: 290+ component sets, 5,300+ variants and states and 500+ design tokens. I helped build and document its components, states and page patterns, so Search, Orders, CRM, Collections, Presentations and POD feel like one product.

Before bringing AI in, we spent six weeks cleaning up the Sketch-era library, so there was something worth generating from.

One product team, six features in a quarter:

  • Brief to clickable prototype: 11 → 3.5 days.
  • Review rounds per feature: 3.4 → 1.3.
  • Rework after handoff: 4.2 → 1.1 developer-days.
  • Defects per feature set: 19 → 7.
  • Requirement changes after sign-off: −73% (5.1 → 1.4).
  • Zero off-system components in generated UI.

How we measured: over three months across six features on one product team. The client tracked these numbers and their design leadership shared them with us; we had no direct access to the analytics. Results reflect the work of the whole product team.

Fundraising became one switch in store settings, not a new step in the store wizard. Cosmos gave two brands one consistent language. And the team’s role shifted from drawing screens to engineering prototypes, with Figma kept alongside for review and as a copy of every screen.

Build a decision loop that survives busy calendars. Each month my team got a scope. I shaped solutions with my product manager, we reviewed them inside the team, and then they went to the central design critique. We had weekly syncs with our design lead, and they worked. When calendars filled up and the syncs were cancelled for weeks, some decisions stalled in critique, and I worked around it one feature at a time.

Today I would set up an async loop as a backup: a short decision request with two options and my recommendation, a quick video walkthrough, and a default: if there is no objection by an agreed day, the team goes ahead. And I would agree decision rights with the design lead and my product manager from the start. Then the team spends its energy on users, not on alignment.
