Leading design at Valor Software

From 2019 I led designers at Valor Software, and from November 2021 as Head of Design. This is how I built the team, kept it together when it shrank, and what I would tell a new Head of Design.

How I lead

Nine short chapters, from the daily rhythm of the team to the first three months in the role.

Six product designers, no layer of sub-leads, everyone worked directly with me. Each of them covered the full product cycle: user interviews and UX research, journey maps, information architecture, wireframes, prototyping and high-fidelity UI mockups.

We had daily design syncs, a weekly critique and regular workshops. I kept designing my own product and covered others during vacations and sick leave, so I knew every project from the inside.

I also made the case to management for an After Effects course for the team, and Valor paid for it. We did not finish the whole course, but everyone learned the basics. One designer went much further and built complex motion, so we brought her into every project that needed it.

I do not use take-home tests. Instead I worked with our recruiters on real examples of strong and weak candidates, and together we sharpened what we were looking for. At the start about 80% of the candidates were graphic designers, so we went through the reasons for each rejection together. After a few rounds the screening was sharp, and the recruiters became true partners in hiring.

I screened portfolios myself: real shipped work over showcase shots, and I asked for Figma access to check components, structure and auto layout. In the interview I brought a designer from the team. We reviewed the portfolio, checked every claim on the CV, and asked how the person handles conflicting stakeholders and disagreements with PMs. English was checked too.

Result: about 12 designers hired.

In the early years we ran goal-based reviews, knowledge tests and one-to-ones. Later the team started reviews themselves, and I stayed in daily contact with everyone.

A junior with an art background became a solid mid-level designer. A mid-level designer left as an independent senior.

Figma became the one standard, with Dev Mode and prototypes for transitions and animation. Each product had its own design system, at minimum a UI kit with usage guidelines.

In Notion we kept handoff guides, how to request design work, and team availability and status, so everyone knew the rules of the game.

Budget cuts hit and the team shrank sharply. People I had worked with for years left. The work still had to ship, and one of the biggest blockers was not capacity but silence: feedback from a key stakeholder could take weeks.

With the project managers we agreed on a one-week window. If no feedback arrives, the team decides and moves on. We also settled design arguments with evidence instead of taste, using analytics, heatmaps and A/B tests.

Decisions got faster, and silence stopped acting as a veto.

On Mabrook, Oleksandra, a designer I had worked with at Valor, was the only designer when the product started to grow. She asked for me to join. We saw that the product needed more designers, I ran two interviews and hired a senior product designer, and the team grew to three.

“He leads with trust rather than control, which is why people bring him with them from project to project.”Oleksandra Nadtochii, Product Designer

I built a Claude system for recurring internal work. The method: feed Claude real tasks, turn what works into skills, build agents from the skills, document everything in Notion and share it with the design team.

Each designer works with an agent on their project, and six shared agents cover requirements and documentation, presentations, social posts, articles and covers, the Valor site and designer portfolios. A B2C feature that took 5 to 7 working days now sometimes takes 3. Every designer became the lead of a two-person team: themselves and their agent.

Results on client work: Mabrook feature release from 10 to 4 days with AI-assisted design, and ASI Central brief to clickable prototype from 11 to 3.5 days. Figma stays the source of truth.

Together with a colleague I ran two internal masterclasses on AI for the company, mostly for developers: a talk of about two hours with Q&A. I covered Figma AI, and he showed working with Claude.

I lead with authority, not control. I protect the team from outside pressure, and I do the work alongside them.

To a new Head of Design I would say three things:

  • Earn authority. Be useful before you demand anything: help with a real problem, give advice that works, stand up for your people.
  • Make the rules visible. Write down how work is requested, handed off and reviewed, so nobody has to guess.
  • Stay close to the work. A lead who designs is a lead the team trusts.
